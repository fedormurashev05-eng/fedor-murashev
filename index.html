About me

I am a high school senior applying to the BA in Culture, Politics and Society at Central European University.

I have a strong interest in how economic conditions, culture and politics shape relationships between migrants and local communities. This interest grew out of my own experience: at thirteen I moved from Russia to Georgia and had to adapt to a new country. Over five field seasons with the Gnezdovo Archaeological Expedition, I worked on a multi-ethnic settlement of the Viking Age. I also work on a project that prepares commentary by RANEPA experts on social and economic issues for the media, and I take part in school debates on socio-political topics.

Languages: Russian (native), English (IELTS Academic scheduled for December 2026), Georgian (A2).

Writing

Letter of Motivation — Central European University

When I was thirteen, my family moved from Moscow to Tbilisi. For the first time I felt like an outsider. During football matches, boys from other teams called us “Russians” and blamed us for their problems. Even then, my parents explained that it was about old historical tensions, not about me. In Moscow, visiting my grandmothers, I saw migrant couriers being insulted for “taking jobs” from locals. In Tbilisi, I am the outsider; in Moscow, they are. So my question appeared: why do societies see newcomers as a threat?

I look for the answer not only in my own experience. Since 2025, I have worked part-time organizing comments from RANEPA experts for the media, including on labor migration. In my school debate club, we argued about migration and tolerance, and I learned to understand my opponent first and only then answer.

Five seasons at the Gnezdovo archaeological expedition, where Scandinavians, Slavs and Balts lived side by side in the Middle Ages, led me to the question of how different cultures live together. That is why I am attracted to the Cultural Heritage major.

In CPS, it is important for me that society is studied from different sides and students learn to test hypotheses with data, while Experiential Learning projects let me do this in practice. I expect to meet students at CEU whose experience is similar to mine, which will give me more real material for research on adaptation.

I believe that knowledge in the social sciences loses less value from the mass spread of AI, so I want to get a strong foundation at CEU and try to answer the question: why are a teenager on a football field and a courier on the street seen first of all as strangers? And can this be changed?

Essay — Economic anxiety and migration

The main challenge I see today is how economic anxiety turns newcomers into a “threat”. This is happening in Europe, in Russia and in Georgia, where I live. When I was thirteen, my parents and I moved to Tbilisi together with many other Russians. Rent went up very quickly, largely because of newcomers like us, and tension appeared between locals and newcomers. Many Russians started going to “their own” cafés and almost did not communicate with locals. This tension has historical roots, but in my opinion the sharp rise in prices made it much worse.

While I was researching universities, I found an article by CEU professor Anil Duman, “Feeling insecure and excluding immigrants: Relationship between subjective risks and welfare chauvinism” (2023). Using European Social Survey data from European countries, it shows that people more often want to limit migrants’ access to benefits and public services when they feel that their well-being is under threat. At the same time, the feeling of risk does not always match a person’s real situation: even well-off people, if they feel insecure, tend to support excluding migrants. In Central and Eastern Europe this effect is stronger.

Tbilisi is different from the cases in the article: many newcomers were richer than the locals and did not apply for benefits. But I think the main idea of the article fits here too: what matters is not only people’s real situation, but also their feeling of threat. That is why I became interested in where the line is between the real consequences of migration and the subjective perception of threat. To what extent is a negative attitude towards newcomers explained by objective changes, and to what extent by the fear of losing a familiar environment? I would like to learn how to research such questions using data, not only my own observations.

To understand this problem, it is necessary to take into account economic, political and cultural factors. This is exactly why I am attracted to the interdisciplinary approach of the Culture, Politics and Society program. I want to learn how to compare different explanations of social processes and test my own assumptions with scientific methods.

Within the Experiential Learning projects, I would like to study which city initiatives really help people from different groups to communicate instead of closing themselves in their own bubbles. For example, sport: on the football field I saw that the game can both divide people and bring them together. I am interested in understanding what decides which side will be stronger.

Integration does not work if the worries of both sides, newcomers and locals, are not taken into account. This is true not only for Tbilisi, but also for Vienna, Moscow and many other cities. Studying CPS will give me the knowledge and tools to explore this seriously.
